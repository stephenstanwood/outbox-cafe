Bottom rail's the fat one. Put the fat one down first. Bottom rail is always the fat one, because the heavy timber down low keeps the middle of the whole thing low, and the bottom rail is the one nobody hits anyway. Hemi says if you're picking up the heaviest rail last you've built it backwards.

Now: a rail sitting in a cup falls. You nudge it, it drops, that's it, nothing else moves. A rail pinned solid does not fall. It can't. What actually decides it

Footprint against height. Get it taller than about twice how deep the base is, and it doesn't shed a rail — it goes over as a whole unit. Going over as a unit is the one everybody's trying not to have.

Where it's hit. It's not the weight coming at it, it's the moment about the contact point. A rail smacked high by a chest and a rail scuffed low by a hoof are two completely different sums, and a pin sized for one is the wrong pin for the other.

Brush isn't solid. It's drag. The horse goes through the top of it on purpose. So the solid part gets built to the height you want jumped, and the brush goes above that.

Sloping face. Same height, jumped better and hit softer, because the arc and the face are closer to parallel. Vertical is the mean one.

Ground line. Changes nothing mechanical. Changes everything about what it looks like from down there. Base set forward of the top face and it reads honestly; nothing at the bottom and it gets misjudged low every single time, by better horses than the one in my hand.

How to work it

Drag a piece out of the pile at the left, drop it on the lid. It's got real weight. If you stack it stupid it will fall over before you ever flick anything at it.

Pins: pick 11 mm or 13 mm, then tap a rail end that's sitting on an upright. Pin seats with a tink. Sheared pins are gone. Eleven in the bucket and no more.

The flick: drag back from the horse and let go. How far back is speed, the direction is your line, and how far up the horse's body you grabbed sets how it goes up. Grab it high, it tips and tumbles and hits high. Grab it low, it skims flat and scuffs.

Corner box shows the fence's silhouette from down at approach height. That's what it looks like coming at it. Not what it looks like from up here.
