A WORD ABOUT YOUR CAVE
Welcome, friends, and thank you for stopping! While you're waiting on your order, here is a little about the cave under this floor. Folks always want to know why it is 57 degrees down there. It is 57 degrees because 57 degrees is the average air temperature of the hill on top of it, all year averaged out, and that is the whole reason — it isn't a feature we put in, it's just what the hill reads. Humidity runs 97 to 99 percent every day of the year, which is very hard on equipment; we have replaced the amplifier in the Cathedral twice in six years and we have quit trying, so your guide's tour is given by voice and she will be glad to repeat anything for you. Ask her! Now here is the thing I am proudest of. In 1983 we moved every lamp in the cave back from the walls and re‑aimed them so no beam lands square on wet rock inside a metre, and we dropped the Cathedral bank from 750 watts to 300. The lamps look badly aimed. They are aimed correctly. The green is gone off the Cathedral wall and has stayed gone.
The green is called lampenflora — algae, moss and cyanobacteria that come in on shoes and on the air and grow on any lit wet surface. It is our single biggest maintenance item, ahead of the elevator. Every fourth week two of us go through with bleach and a brush on a rotation, and the reason there is a rotation at all is the lighting. Lint is our second one, and it is not a joke: cotton fibre and hair off of people settles onto wet flowstone, mats down, holds water, feeds the algae and stains for good. In January and February we are on our hands and knees at stops 6 and 8 with tweezers and a squeeze bottle. Third, and you can see this one yourself at stop 3: every person breathes out about 40 grams of carbon dioxide an hour, it dissolves in the water film on the rock, and that weak acid takes calcite back off the popcorn. Look at the popcorn at chest and nose height along the rail, then look at the same formation eight feet up. It is dulled at rail height and not dulled above it, and it tracks how many people we run and nothing else. Please don't touch, and please do look.
A few more answers, since I have the room. Tours leave 22 minutes apart. That interval is not the length of the tour — it is set by the narrowest place two groups can pass, which is a 31‑inch gap at the top of the Long Stairs, and a guide who hurries only makes a pile‑up there, so let her take her time. The screen door on the ticket window bangs by itself before a storm; that is the cave breathing out on a falling barometer, and on a rising one the entrance culvert fogs. On a hot August afternoon, 180 people at about 100 watts apiece plus the lamps will genuinely put a degree and a half into the air near the entrance, and it takes till morning to come back down. A soda straw grows about a tenth of a millimetre a year — an inch a century — so the stubs at stop 8 that were snapped off between 1928 and 1953 are snapped for good, and we light them on purpose. Coins in the Cold Pool are copper and copper is bad in that water; we go in with a magnet and a net. And the names — Bridal Chamber, Frozen Niagara, Wedding Cake — were written by Mr. Arliss Ploog selling tickets in 1931. They are advertising, not geology, and they have sold a great many tickets. The 148 steps are why there is an elevator, and the elevator is why the ticket is four seventy‑five. See you at two o'clock! — Dessa Runion‑Ploog
