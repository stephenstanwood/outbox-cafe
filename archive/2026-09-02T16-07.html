Right — the thing about a payout percentage, and I want to say this before Sekiguchi comes up, because he doesn't believe me: it isn't a setting. There's no dial. Nobody types eighty-seven into anything. Eighty-seven is the angle of two hundred and forty brass nails and nothing else on this earth. You want it at eighty-seven, you bend nails until it's at eighty-seven. That's the whole job. So when he says "just put it back to spec," there's no spec to put it back to, there's a field of nails and a count.

And you can't tell by looking. That's the honest bit. A ball coming off the rail is deterministic in principle, sure, it's a lump of steel and there's no magic, but every nail it grazes turns a difference the width of a hair into a difference the width of your thumb by two rows down, and by the time it's at the bottom your thumb is a lane. So the only way to know what a board does is fire several thousand balls at it and count them. That's what the figure on my sheet is doing right now — it's counting. Watch the spread narrow. It narrows and then it stops narrowing much and that's when you can believe it.

Eleven days is nothing, by the way. Eleven days on one machine is barely a number. I said that to Sekiguchi on Saturday and he said "it's four percent, Nishizuka," and I said four percent of eleven days is still eleven days, and he said the shutters go up at ten. Which they do. So here I am.

Now — Sekiguchi wants me to close the throat. The throat's those two nails sat right over the middle pocket, one either side, and if you tap them in a hair the pocket gets narrower and fewer balls drop and the figure comes down. And he wants that because you can see a closed throat. He can stand at the end of the aisle and go, right, something's been done. And I have told him three times now: closing a throat is the first thing anybody checks. It is the first thing. It's the first thing I'd check.

Because taps are the cost. That's the thing nobody outside this floor knows. Every tap loosens the nail in the plywood a bit, and past about six the hole is enlarged and the nail starts moving on its own under ball impact, and then your machine drifts off figure inside a week and there is nothing you can do about it, ever, because you cannot un-enlarge a hole. That's why you work in as few taps as you can. Not because it's elegant. Because the wood remembers.

Bend a nail up and the ball bounces higher off it and carries further. Bend it down and you kill the bounce and it drops nearly straight. They're not opposites though — a ball's going slower up top than it is down at the bottom, so the same bend does two different things at two different heights. Up top you're steering. Down low you're stopping.

And the balls aren't the same as each other either. Worn ball's lighter and rounder and runs faster and further than a fresh one. The tray's mixed — that's normal, stock gets mixed — so your identical shot isn't identical, ever. You can see it, the worn ones sit brighter.
