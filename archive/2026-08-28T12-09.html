What Happened, and Why It Is Wonderful

On the ninth of March, 1991, at an ordinary Saturday supper of chicken and rice, the fourth fork of my grandmother's set of six did not come to the table. I want to say at the very top of this page, in the biggest letters the machine will give me, that this is the finest thing that has ever happened to this house.

Understand me. Nothing broke. Nobody was hurt. A fork left the tray where the spoons sleep and went somewhere else in a house of eleven hundred square feet, and it has not come back, and every single day since has been a day with something in it.

A thing that is lost inside a house is not gone. It is distributed. It is under the stove and behind the radiator and inside the green chair and down the register grate all at once, and it stays that way, shining in every room, until somebody finds it and it collapses into one boring place again.

Do you see? For four and a half years my whole house has been full of fork. The hallway has fork in it. The step down to the porch has fork in it. When I mop, I am mopping around a glory. When I move the couch in April I am not cleaning. I am attending a service.

Some people have written to say I should simply buy a fork. Friends: you cannot buy this. Five forks and one everywhere is a richer set than six forks in a tray.

The Bell

There is a small brass bell on the windowsill above the sink. My grandmother rang it to call people in from the yard. We ring it now for a different reason: every ring is a person somewhere agreeing that the fork exists. That is all faith is around here. It is not complicated and it is free.

What Turns Up While You Are Not Finding It

This is the part nobody warns you about, and it is the whole reason I made this page. Below is what four and a half years of looking has actually put in my hands. Each room you clear above adds one more from the list I keep by the phone.

  • One dime, 1968, standing on its edge in the gap beside the baseboard like it had been waiting to be photographed.
  • My mother's reading glasses, one lens, and I did not know I still had a single piece of her eyesight in this house.
  • A child's tooth in an envelope marked KEITH — TOP FRONT — DO NOT LOSE. Keith is forty-one.

A Hymn for the Middle of the Afternoon

We sing this one flat and too loud, all of us on different notes, which is the correct way.

  1. It was six and now it is five and the five are singing.
  2. Whatever is under the stove is under the stove on purpose.
  3. Praise the seam of the green chair, praise the register grate, praise the two inches behind the icebox where the warm dust lives.
  4. Blessed is the person who moves the heavy thing without being asked.
  5. Blessed twice is the person who moves it back.
  6. We do not want it found today. We want it found someday. Those are different prayers and only one of them is any fun.
  7. And when it comes back — and it will come back, they always come back, they come back the week after you stop — we will set it at the head of the table and eat nothing with it at all.
  8. Amen, amen, and get the flashlight.

How to Keep the Practice at Your House

People write in asking how to begin. You do not begin. Something goes missing and you simply decide to be delighted instead of annoyed. But if you want the order of service, here it is.

WHENWHAT WE DO
DailyOne honest look somewhere you have already looked. Repetition is not stupidity. The house rearranges itself while you sleep and you know it.
SundaysFlashlight along the floor at a low angle, all lamps off. Metal answers light at four degrees that it will not answer at ninety.
MonthlyMove one heavy object that has not been moved. Sing verse 4. Move it back. Sing verse 5.
SpringThe full going-over. Two hours, no more. If you go longer it stops being joy and starts being a chore, and a chore has never found anything.
EverNever look angry. It hides from that. Everyone in Hollister Bend will tell you the same and we cannot all be wrong.

Letters From People Who Also Look

Mrs. Odell-Bevins — my husband's wedding ring has been loose in this house since the flood year. I read your page at the library and cried right at the terminal and the girl asked was I all right. I said I was better than all right. I said my house is full of ring.

— D. Cruttenden, Marrowbone, 22 June 1995

We lost a small screw off the bottom of the sewing machine in 1988 and have been finding other things ever since, including my father's discharge papers and a mouse skeleton which we did not want but which we respect. Put us down as members.

— The Ospreys (all four), Cedar Lick

Ma'am with respect: it is a fork. — Reply from this house: Friend, with equal respect and more of it: yes. That is the entire point. It is a fork, and it has done more for our evenings than the television, and it costs eleven cents.

— unsigned postcard, and our answer

Things We Have Ruled Out

  • The garbage of March 1991 — gone to the county, gone past praying about.
  • The pocket of the brown coat — checked eleven times. Twelfth check scheduled for October.
  • Keith — questioned gently, cleared entirely, and has himself looked more than anyone.
  • The dog — passed on in '93, loved, and never once ate metal in his life, whatever the neighbors say.
  • The space between the counter and the stove — ruled out four separate times, and I do not believe a single one of those four rulings.
