1. SNOW — FALLING FLAKE, SOLD BY THE POUND

Terminal velocity is what sells a flake. A flake that comes down faster than about 1.4 feet per second reads as confetti from row K no matter what shape you cut it, and I have sat in row K of the Convention Hall here and watched it happen. Bulk density is the other half of the arithmetic, because coverage per pound is meaningless without it — a competitor's book this year quotes "600 square feet per pound" for a flake and does not print a density anywhere in ninety-six pages, which is not a figure, it is a decoration. My densities below are weighed in a one-cubic-foot box, three fills, averaged.

Density on a school hall stage: 1.5 flakes per square foot per second is a light fall you can see from the back; 3 is a good storm; anything above 6 is a blizzard and your actors cannot be heard over the sweeping afterwards. The pound figure is honest — it comes off the flake's own weight per flake and its own fall rate, and it does not know or care what the flake costs. The cheap flake is very often the expensive order.

2. RAIN — RIGS, HEADS, PUMPS, TROUGHS

A rain rig is a pipe with holes in it, a pump, and a trough to catch what comes down; the trough is not the optional part. Every December, without fail, I get telephone calls from theatres who bought the pipe and the pump and skipped the catch trough, and there is nothing I can do for them on the fourteenth of the month except tell them what I am telling you now in October. Order the trough. Most rigs sold in this country have never had water through them once, which is the cheapest way to own one but not the reason to buy it.

3. WIND, THUNDER, LOW FOG

The wind machine is a slatted drum turned against a tensioned canvas apron. Drum speed sets the pitch and canvas tension sets the volume, and those two adjustments are independent, so a crank hand can hold a pitch and swell it, which is the only reason to use a drum instead of a loudspeaker. Thunder sheet must hang free at one edge — clamped at two edges it clangs like a shop door and will not rumble. Dry-ice fog sits low because it is cold; on a raked deck it runs downhill, off the apron, and pools in the pit, so figure eight to twelve minutes of dwell before it is where you want it and put a fan on the upstage side if your rake is over one in twenty-four.

THE NEW CRADLE HANGER (SC-3H)

The hanger is new this year and it is worth the three dollars. The old yoke bolted the trough rigid to the batten, so the shake had to come out of the operator's wrists and the rhythm went to pieces after ninety seconds; the new hanger carries the trough on two hardened pins with a half-inch of free swing fore and aft. The trough now returns itself, so the crank hand is only feeding the swing rather than making it, and a steady two-per-second shake holds for the length of a scene. That is the whole of the improvement and it is entirely mechanical.

4. SHIPPING & CRATING

Freight is charged on dimensional weight or actual weight, whichever is greater, at 194 cubic inches to the pound. Snow flake is bulk with almost nothing in it, so forty pounds of flake in its crate bills like a hundred and ten; forty pounds of pump housing bills like forty. That is not our rule and I cannot waive it. Crates ship whole. An eleven-pound order of flake goes in the same 25-lb crate as a twenty-five-pound order and costs the same to freight, so if you are near a crate line, fill the crate.
